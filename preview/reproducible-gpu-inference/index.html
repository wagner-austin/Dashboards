Reproducible GPU inference

← Austin Wagner

One model and one set of weights, run on nine NVIDIA cards, produced nine different sets of numbers. Closing that gap took three interventions, and the first two were not enough.

Measurements dated 2026-08-26 to 2026-09-05. Each figure below names the run that produced it; the source-verification section at the end carries the chain.

The observation

A GPT-2 cloze scorer was run over a 2,627-item set on several GPUs under a pinned environment: one content-addressed container image, one driver, one PyTorch build, and PyTorch's determinism switches enabled. The cards agreed on every decision the scorer made, and the accuracy was identical everywhere. Not one of the 2,627 per-item scores was bit-identical across any two cards.

Determinism settings of the usual kind address a different problem. They make one card reproduce itself. Nothing in that set makes two different cards agree with each other, and the distinction is the whole subject.

The first explanation was wrong

The disagreement appeared above a certain model size, which suggested a threshold — some point where a larger computation begins to diverge. That reading survived until individual matrix multiplications were run in isolation, at the same dimensions and through the same library entry point, with operands generated on the CPU and copied so that every card multiplied identical bits, and with the whole output tensor fingerprinted instead of a single loss.

Six of the eight shapes produced three different tensors on three cards, including three rungs whose reported losses were bit-identical on all three. The cards were never computing alike at those sizes. The difference was present and did not survive the reduction to a scalar — it sat below one float32 unit in the last place of the loss by the time it arrived there.

The threshold was misnamed. It is not the size at which cards begin to disagree. It is the size at which a disagreement already present grows large enough to move the last bit of a number a person reads.

Intervention one: the vendor's reduction shortcut

cuBLASLt offers a strategy called split-K, which partitions the summed dimension of a matrix multiply into partial results and recombines them. Reordering a floating-point sum changes it, and the partition count is chosen per device. Setting CUBLASLT_WORKSPACE_SIZE=0 removes split-K from the available options: measured locally, 70 of 86 matmul calls used it by default and none did with the workspace at zero.

Re-running the eight isolated shapes on three cards with that one variable changed moved agreement from 2 of 8 shapes to 8 of 8, bit for bit. Removing the mechanism removed the entire effect, which makes it a demonstration by intervention rather than a correlation across eight samples.

On a whole model it did much less. The rung-level count over real GPT-2 forward passes moved only from three of eight rungs disagreeing to two of eight, and one gate rung changed value on two of three cards.

That count was a bad instrument, not a disappointing result. Digesting every tensor at every module boundary, rather than the final loss, showed the same switch taking two Ampere cards from agreeing on 1–4% of the forward pass to agreeing on over 99.9% of it. A count of agreeing losses is too coarse to say what the switch did.

What the controls alone were worth

Split-K never touched attention, because scaled-dot-product attention is not a cuBLASLt call — not one of 72 measured attention digests moved when split-K was removed. A second control pins attention to its math backend, which is bit-identical across cards but materialises the full score matrix, so its cost grows with the square of sequence length.

With both controls applied, real pretrained GPT-2 at its full 50,257-token vocabulary scored 150 cloze items on four cards:

The denominator is 150 items across all six pairs of four cards, so 900 is a count of pairwise comparisons rather than a count of test items. Both rows have the controls applied: the controls alone take 198 of 900 comparisons to exact agreement, and the rest need something else. Accuracy, correct count and decisions were identical under both arms throughout. Only the scores differed.

Intervention two: owning the reduction order

The remaining gap closes by not asking the vendor library for the sum at all. A fixed-order accumulation over the summed dimension — K passes over an M×N accumulator — produces the same arithmetic on every card because the order is the program's rather than the device's.

On the full 2,627-item set, sharded into 18 chunks that reassemble byte-identically:

Arm (both controls applied)Per-item scores bit-identical, per card pair
Vendor cuBLAS156 – 1,650 of 2,627, depending on the pair
Owned reduction order2,627 / 2,627 on all six pairs

The confirmation is not a statistic. The per-item outcomes file — every item and every candidate score — is byte-identical across the cards under the owned arm: one SHA-256, 7112cf716db61270…, written by six cards spanning sm_70 through sm_89 and two operating systems. Under the vendor arm the same file has a different hash on every card.

The arm is computing rather than doing nothing: within every single card, the owned scores differ from the vendor arm's on all 150 items. The accuracy is unchanged at 1,374 of 2,627 on every card under both arms. The arms change bits, never answers.

The card that broke it

A GTX 1630 — sm_75, a Turing card the cluster could not supply — returned 147 of 150, the first card to break the fixed-order identity, by at most 8.7e-05 on three items, with decisions still agreeing on all 150.

The three divergent items were not the long ones; one was the shortest in the set. Every owned stage was identical across cards, and what differed sat in the arithmetic the arm had deliberately left to the vendor. A stage probe taking attention apart at the exact sequence lengths involved named the operation: the value product, probs @ v, at 15- and 16-token sequences, with the query-key product and the softmax exonerated cell by cell, and lengths 17, 20 and 64 clean everywhere.

Owning that operation as well closed the card. The fully-owned scorer produced a single SHA-256 across the GTX 1630, an RTX 3090 Ti and an RTX 3070 Ti Laptop, and then 2,627 of 2,627 on the full set. The card whose vendor-arm run had agreed with the cluster on as few as 156 items scored the entire set byte-for-byte.

nine cards · six architecture families · two operating systems · two CUDA toolchains

What it costs

Removing split-K costs between 0% and 13% per matrix multiply at a realistic batch size, several of the measured figures being negative. That number required correcting an earlier measurement that argued for the wrong decision.

The first cost table was taken at a single 64-token sequence and showed penalties up to +84%. Sweeping the batch dimension at every doubling from 64 to 4096 rows shows why that was the wrong place to measure:

The cost collapses between 64 and 128 rows and never returns. One doubling. 128 rows is two 64-token sequences, a quarter of a single 512-token sequence, so any workload resembling training is already past the knee. The original table had measured the one batch size on the curve where the cost exists.

At 64 rows only three of sixteen measurements cleared the per-call overhead floor, where dispatch and kernel launch dominate and arithmetic cannot be resolved at all. At 4096 rows every shape clears it. The batched table is both the more realistic one and the better measured one.

Three conclusions that were withdrawn

Each of these was published inside the project's own record before being measured properly and retracted. They are kept because the corrections are more reusable than the results.

  • "The gate rung regresses." Disabling split-K appeared to break a registered known-answer rung. A forward trace the next day showed that rung's three cards had never agreed on its computation under either condition — 42 of its 52 module-boundary tensors already differed by default. What ended was a coincidence in the last bit of one scalar, not an agreement. The number stood; the word did not.
  • "Reproducibility costs up to 84%." True only at a batch size nobody trains at, and quoted as the headline figure by an earlier revision. The batch sweep above replaced it.
  • "No known-answer registry exists." Concluded after four exhaustive searches — the api repository, two cluster filesystems and the collected artifacts — none of them in the repository the file was actually in, which the same session had written it to hours earlier. A duplicate registry was built on that conclusion and has since been merged back and deleted. Absence is the hardest thing to establish, and an exhaustive search of one namespace is not evidence about the others.

What the result does not establish

A run with no divergences bounds a rate. It does not establish zero, and there is no spread to divide by, so the usual effect-size instruments return a misleadingly perfect answer. The exact Clopper-Pearson bound 1 - 0.05^(1/n) is the right one.

Measured against the scale this work operates at — one divergence in a full 2,627-item run is 0.0381% — none of those rows excludes it. The repeat axis is weaker still: bit-identity across a corpus says nothing about whether a fresh pair of runs reproduces it, and that was observed once. The identity claims stand exactly as stated, and what they bound is narrower than the phrase "bit-identical" suggests on its own.
